The experiment

Our question is simple: if two identical thermometers are covered by two different coatings, do they react differently to sunlight and shadow in space?

What we measure

The board carries two MCP9808 digital thermometers of the same type. One is covered with black epoxy (Strongbond 53), the other with a layer of clear epoxy about 2 millimetres thick (Strongbond 110).

When the satellite comes out of the Earth's shadow into sunlight, the board starts to warm up, and when it enters the shadow, it cools down. These temperature transients are the most interesting parts of the data. Our hypothesis was that the two coatings absorb light and give off heat differently. If so, the difference should show up here, and this is what we want to investigate in orbit.

The thermometer under black epoxy is the reference, the one under clear epoxy is the sample we study. We follow how the difference between the two changes from the beginning to the end of the mission. From this we hope to learn about the thermal inertia of the coatings and about possible UV ageing of the clear epoxy.

The third sensor is an OPT3002 light sensor. It shows when the Sun shines on the board and when it is in shadow, so we can mark the shadow entries and exits on the temperature curves.

The board

Size45 × 30 × 3 mm
Mass5.5 g
Location on the satelliteHUNITY's deployable experiment panel
Thermometers2 × MCP9808 digital thermometer (one under black epoxy, one under ~2 mm clear epoxy)
Light sensorOPT3002
MicrocontrollerSTM32F103 (C8T6)
Design toolsKiCad (PCB), STM32CubeIDE (firmware)
Samplingmostly every 60 seconds; every 10 seconds in two sessions in January 2026 and since September 2026

The MCP9808 is a digital thermometer IC: it measures the temperature itself and passes a ready-made value to the microcontroller. It is not a thermocouple.

How it was built

We designed the PCB in KiCad and wrote the firmware in STM32CubeIDE. The board has a separate connector for programming and debugging, and another one through which it talks to the satellite. We tested the first programs on a breadboard with the parts wired together.

What we would do differently today

The microcontroller ended up closer to the thermometer under black epoxy than to the one under clear epoxy, so the small amount of heat it gives off while running does not warm the two thermometers equally. We would also put a timestamp in the measurement records, so that the time of each measurement would not have to be reconstructed afterwards. And we would make the two coatings the same size, so that only their material would differ.

Testing on the ground

We also tested the finished boards in extreme conditions. In December 2024 we put them in an industrial freezer in a laboratory of the Hungarian University of Agriculture and Life Sciences (MATE), where the lowest temperature was −35.5 °C. We also tested the boards in an oven at 100 °C. We compared the sensor readings with calibrated instruments, and measured the board's mass and its current draw at idle and at maximum load, to show that it meets the satellite's requirements.

While testing the light sensor we also answered an old question: yes, the light inside the fridge really does turn off when you close the door.

The board in orbit has a reference copy on the ground. It lets us check whether certain features of the data from space come from the board's own operation.
